# Research Writing: Source Notes

These notes record how YU's guides and writing notes informed the skill. The
original PDFs are not bundled; page numbers refer to the supplied files.

## PDF guides

**`does-my-writing-flow.pdf`**: *Does My Writing Flow?*, The Writing Center,
edited December 2017, two pages. Page 1 uses a reverse outline to connect the
central claim, paragraph points, and supporting evidence. Page 2 recommends
headings as a drafting aid and transitions that reflect the relationship
between ideas. The adaptation avoids a rigid topic-sentence template.

**`Method写作常见问题.pdf`**: Six pages, with paper excerpts on pages 4–5.
Page 1 identifies unclear organization, missing motivation or overview,
misplaced emphasis, excessive notation, and unexplained value. Pages 2–3
address the sequence and emphasis of ideas; page 4 discusses notation; page 5
contrasts starting with details against giving an overview. Page 6 checks the
line of thought, paragraph purpose, sentence motivation, flow, and consistent
terms, including “一段文字一定要表达好一件事情.” The skill adapts these
points without removing necessary notation or reproducibility details.

## YU's writing notes

- **Titles and abstracts:** Start titles from meaningful task, problem, and
  technique keywords. For abstracts, work out the challenge, contribution,
  why it works, advantage, and new insight. YU prefers introducing the insight
  before the technical contribution when the material supports it.
- **Introductions:** Plan backward, then write forward (**倒推，然后正推**).
  For a new setting, introduce the broader application before the setting.
  State the challenge early when natural, but give prior work enough context
  when needed. Present the actual contribution rather than an artificial
  sequence of patches to a naive solution.
- **Technical challenges:** The notes distinguish established tasks with prior
  methods, earlier versions of an insight, and new tasks or settings. Explain
  limitations with their technical causes and credit related work. The
  [storyline reference](../skills/research-writing/references/technical-challenge-storylines.md)
  retains the patterns and supplied example papers.
- **Pipeline introduction:** YU supplied patterns for one contribution with
  multiple advantages, related contributions, a new module in an existing
  pipeline, and an observation-led contribution. Explain the actual steps,
  including for simple pipelines, rather than relying on abstract or novel
  labels. Use a real figure reference and separate intended benefits from
  observed results.
- **Methods:** Sketch the pipeline, organize subsections around its modules,
  and distinguish each module's motivation, design, and technical advantages.
  Draft the design first, then add the other elements. The final order can
  differ. The overview connects the setting and core contribution to the
  figure and module roadmap.
- **Experiments:** Plan relevant comparisons, component and design-choice
  ablations, and applications or demos. Captions should identify settings and
  notation. YU prefers single-column figures and tables on the right of a
  two-column layout when practical. A demo shows capability in its conditions;
  it does not by itself establish general performance or an upper bound.
- **Related work and conclusion:** Discuss the closest papers first, then
  organize them by relevant topics. Consider meaningful limits of the task or
  setting. The linked [limitations discussion](https://github.com/pengsida/learning_research/issues/12)
  compares task goals and customary metrics; the adaptation also reports
  material technical limitations and tradeoffs accurately.
- **Revision and evidence:** For substantive paper revision, use a self-review
  covering contribution, clarity, result strength, evaluation completeness,
  and method design. Check claims against appropriate evidence, especially in
  the abstract and introduction, and flag missing support.
- **Tables:** The [table reference](../skills/research-writing/references/research-tables.md)
  consolidates YU's preferences for sparse rules, clear labels and alignment,
  grouping, explicit attributes, captions and notes, selective highlighting,
  and editable output.

The contribution-focused framing also reflects YU's global
`anti-defensive-writing` preference. The skill contains the operational
guidance; these notes document its origins.

## Examples and unavailable material

YU supplied [Seeing a Rose in Five Thousand Ways](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.04965)
and the [NeRF in the Wild CVPR PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Martin-Brualla_NeRF_in_the_Wild_Neural_Radiance_Fields_for_Unconstrained_Photo_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf)
as introduction examples. The skill links to the latter's arXiv copy.

Experiment examples were [NeRF in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.02268)
for comparisons, [NeRF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08934) for component and
sensitivity ablations, [Dynamic MLP Maps](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.06717)
for contribution and design-choice ablations, and
[Dynamic Humans in Minutes](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.12237) for component,
input-design, and parameter ablations.

The linked Method example images and editable illustrations, and the Notion
page on demos and applications, were inaccessible when consulted. The
adaptation uses the supplied text and PDF instead of assuming their contents.
