# Prior-Work and Novelty Assessment

Begin by trying to understand and develop the proposed idea. Similar work is
evidence to analyze, not an automatic reason to reject it.

## Investigate the closest work

1. Specify the proposed problem, setting, central mechanism, and anticipated
   contribution. Mark unresolved details as hypotheses.
2. Search systematically for a set of the closest papers using relevant
   terminology, alternative descriptions, and citation trails. Include strong
   potential overlaps, not only papers that support the proposed story.
3. Prioritize original papers and, where accessible, read their full methods,
   problem settings, technical details, claimed contributions, and relevant
   experiments or appendices. Do not infer a paper's method or contribution
   boundary from its title or abstract alone. When implementation or benchmark
   details matter, inspect relevant released code, datasets, or examples too.
   Record the source or version and distinguish features present in an artifact
   from results or contributions established by the paper.
4. Compare the actual problem, assumptions, key mechanism, contributions,
   and supporting evidence with the proposed idea. Identify what is shared,
   what differs, and whether each difference has meaningful consequences.

Record and cite the sources that support the comparison. If a close paper is
unavailable beyond an abstract or other limited material, say what could not
be inspected and keep the corresponding conclusion provisional. Do not claim
an exhaustive search or absolute originality from an incomplete review.

## Explain a defensible advance

When the evidence supports a meaningful distinction, write a clear, persuasive
novelty paragraph: what the proposed work adds, how it advances the closest
prior work, and why that difference matters. The advance can be a new problem
setting, mechanism, understanding, or other substantive contribution; justify
it from the papers and the proposed work rather than relying on labels.

## Stop when the collision is substantive

Do not manufacture novelty to preserve a direction. If close work already
substantially covers the central problem, key method, and main contribution,
and the remaining differences cannot support a credible distinction, stop
arguing that the original idea is novel. Identify the specific papers, the
overlap, and why the differences are insufficient. Explain any remaining
uncertainty and, where useful, a genuinely distinct next path.
