# Research Writing

Help the reader understand the research, its value, and the reasoning that
connects each part. Adapt to the field, document, audience, and the user's
requested scope. Write in the requested language; otherwise follow the
language and context of the task.

## Working principles

- **Center the strongest supported contribution.** Make the work's specific
  value and the conditions in which it matters clear. Organize the narrative
  around the problem, approach, and evidence rather than the chronological
  history of the project. Match claims and comparisons to the evidence; if
  the original framing exceeds what the results support, narrow or reconsider
  it and make substantive changes visible to the author. Avoid unnecessary
  self-undermining language.
- **Write for the reader.** Provide the background and definitions the
  intended audience needs, and make the relationship between the section's
  purpose, its paragraphs, and their supporting explanations visible.
- **Give each paragraph a job.** Make its main point easy to recognize, keep
  its content relevant to that point, and connect it to the surrounding
  argument. Use topic sentences where they help, without imposing the same
  paragraph pattern everywhere.
- **Make the logic explicit.** Order ideas so each follows from what the reader
  already knows. Use transitions when they clarify a real relationship;
  connecting words alone cannot repair a missing explanation.
- **Allocate detail by importance.** Explain the central ideas and mechanisms
  enough to understand them; compress routine or distracting details where
  doing so does not sacrifice accuracy or necessary reproducibility.
- **Support substantive claims.** Check their accuracy and trace them to
  appropriate evidence, especially in the abstract and introduction. Empirical
  performance, advantage, and robustness claims need relevant experimental
  support under the stated conditions; other claims may require reliable
  sources, derivations, or documented method details. Preserve material
  findings and qualifications, flag unverified claims, and never invent
  evidence or results.

## Apply the guidance

1. Identify the requested deliverable, audience, and available evidence. For an
   excerpt, base the assessment on the supplied context and avoid claiming to
   assess the whole paper.
2. Determine the central claim or purpose of the material and the role of each
   relevant section or paragraph. When developing the paper's story, identify
   the problem, why existing approaches fall short, the contribution, why it
   works, and the supported advantage or insight. A new insight (新认知) is a
   grounded understanding of the problem or why a design can address it;
   naming a method or reporting a result does not necessarily convey one.
   Restructure when needed to make the logic clear, while preserving the
   author's intended contribution and facts.
3. Draft or revise the prose directly when writing is requested. For a review,
   identify the consequential problems, where they occur, and concrete ways
   to fix them. Scale the depth to the request.
4. Check the result for readable flow, consistent terminology, accurate
   support, and an appropriate level of detail.
