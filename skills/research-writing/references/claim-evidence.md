# Claim–Evidence Checks

Check substantive claims throughout the paper, giving particular attention to
the abstract and introduction. A strong claim needs evidence that supports
what it actually says, including its scope and conditions.

## Trace claims to support

1. Identify the substantive claims in the requested material: what the method
   does, why it works, how well it performs, what it improves, and how it
   relates to prior work. Include claims expressed in titles or figure captions
   when relevant.
2. Trace each claim to the appropriate available source. Empirical claims about
   performance, efficiency, robustness, generalization, or effectiveness
   require relevant experiments or measurements. Use the actual tables,
   figures, logs, and evaluation protocols where available.
3. Check that the evidence matches the claim's metric, dataset or population,
   baseline, operating conditions, and reported value. Do not extend a finding
   from a particular test to all settings or convert a correlation into a
   proven causal mechanism without supporting evidence.
4. For background or prior-work claims, check the relevant sources. For a
   mathematical claim, inspect the derivation and assumptions; for a statement
   about the method's design, check the documented implementation or method
   description. Not every such claim requires an experiment.
5. Distinguish **supported**, **contradicted or overbroad**, and **unverified
   with the available material**. A missing source does not establish that a
   claim is false, but it also does not verify the claim.

Describe proxy evidence as measured. Rater agreement alone does not establish
label accuracy, and a simulated evaluation alone does not establish
performance with real users.

## Resolve mismatches

Correct factual errors using verified evidence; narrow an overbroad claim to
the actual results and conditions; or flag a claim that needs evidence or a
new experiment. Do not silently present an unverified claim as established,
manufacture results, or turn a hypothesis into a demonstrated finding. Make
substantive changes visible to the author.

For a focused review, identify the claim, its location, the relevant evidence
or missing source, and the needed correction. For a normal writing task, use
this check in the background and report consequential gaps without adding a
full audit table unless requested. If only an excerpt is available, inspect
the sources that can be accessed and state what remains unverified rather than
claiming that the whole paper has been checked.
