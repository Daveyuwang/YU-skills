# Technical Challenge Storylines

Use these patterns to lead readers from the task and relevant prior work to
the challenge the paper actually addresses. Choose the pattern that fits the
available evidence; the sequences are guides, not fixed paragraph counts.

## 1. Established task with prior methods

1. Introduce the task's general challenges when they help explain why the
   relevant methods were developed.
2. If relevant traditional methods exist, briefly explain what they do and
   achieved, then the limitation they encounter and its technical cause. A
   precise account of the established literature shows understanding of the
   field (**显示我们很懂这个领域**).
3. If useful, discuss one or more recent method families in the same way:
   their approach and value, followed by the remaining limitation and its
   cause. Explain the relationship between families where it matters.
4. Lead to the specific limitation that the paper addresses. Make clear why
   it occurs in the relevant setting and how it motivates the paper's idea.

Traditional and recent families need not each occupy a paragraph. Do not add
families merely for chronology: include them when they advance the argument.

## 2. Established task with an earlier version of the insight

Use this route when the paper's guiding idea has meaningful precedent in a
traditional or earlier method:

1. Explain a relevant approach and the technical challenge it faces.
2. Introduce the earlier method that used the related insight or principle.
   Explain what it did, why the principle was attractive, and the limitation
   and technical reason that remained in its implementation or setting.
3. Where relevant, discuss a later approach and the limitation that leads to
   the paper's specific challenge. Then explain what the paper contributes by
   adapting or developing the idea.

Prior use can make an insight intelligible and provide historical motivation;
it is not evidence that the new method works. Credit the earlier work and
identify the actual new contribution without presenting the established idea
itself as newly discovered.

## 3. New task or setting without a directly established solution

1. State the goal and the specific setting clearly.
2. Identify the requirements or difficulties that make the goal technically
   challenging. For each important one, explain why it arises and what
   constraint or consequence follows. Use as many points as the argument
   needs, not a fixed number.
3. Where relevant and supported, explain why an adjacent method or tool cannot
   be applied directly under those conditions. Then connect the challenges to
   the paper's insight and approach.

A new task or setting can still have related prior work. Describe the precise
gap without assuming that no methods exist anywhere; support claims about
novelty and the literature, or flag them as unresolved to the author.

## Example papers

YU supplied these examples to study how an introduction develops a challenge:

- [Seeing a Rose in Five Thousand Ways](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.04965)
  sets out the goal, explains several constraints of learning from one image,
  and relates them to earlier approaches.
- [NeRF in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.02268) identifies assumptions
  of an established method and explains why those assumptions are challenged
  by an unconstrained setting.

Both discuss relevant earlier work. Use them as illustrations of structure,
not as citations to insert into an unrelated manuscript.

## Across all three

Keep each limitation tied to its technical reason and to the paper's target
challenge. Represent prior approaches accurately, preserve their actual
achievements, and do not invent citations, causes, or comparative results.
Prioritize the reasoning that motivates the contribution over an exhaustive
list of methods.
